Module 1What AI actually is

Lesson 1 of 6 · 10 min read

AI, machine learning and deep learning

“AI” is used for everything from spam filters to self-driving cars, so it helps to start with a simple picture: three nested circles.

  • Artificial intelligence (AI) is the outer circle — any technique that lets a computer do something we would normally call intelligent: recognising speech, recommending a film, answering a question.
  • Machine learning (ML) sits inside it. Instead of a programmer writing every rule, the system learns patterns from examples. Show it thousands of labelled emails and it learns what spam looks like.
  • Deep learning is a kind of machine learning that uses large neural networks with many layers. It is what made modern image recognition, speech recognition and language models possible.

Rules versus learning

Traditional software follows rules: if the order total is over ₹5,000, apply free shipping. Machine learning flips this around. You provide inputs and the desired outputs, and the model works out the pattern. That is powerful when the rules are too complex to write down — like describing what makes a photo “a cat”.

The trade-off is that learned behaviour is probabilistic. A rule always gives the same answer; a model gives its best guess, which is usually right and occasionally wrong. That single fact shapes almost every product decision you will make with AI.

Key takeaways
  • AI ⊃ machine learning ⊃ deep learning.
  • ML learns patterns from examples instead of hand-written rules.
  • Learned behaviour is probabilistic — design for the occasional wrong answer.
Lesson 2 of 6 · 14 min read

How large language models work

A large language model (LLM) — Claude, Gemini, GPT, Llama — is a deep learning model trained on huge amounts of text to do one deceptively simple thing: predict the next piece of text.

Tokens

Models don’t read words; they read tokens — chunks of text that are often a word, part of a word or a punctuation mark. “Unbelievable” might be split into “un”, “believ”, “able”. Tokens matter because they are how models are measured and priced: context limits and API bills are counted in tokens.

Training versus inference

  • Training is the expensive, one-time phase where the model learns from data. Most models are then refined with human feedback so they follow instructions and behave helpfully.
  • Inference is what happens every time you send a prompt: the model reads your tokens and generates new ones, one at a time, each chosen based on everything before it.

The context window

The context window is how much text the model can consider at once — your instructions, any documents you attach, the conversation so far and its own reply. Modern frontier models handle hundreds of thousands to around a million tokens. Anything outside the window, the model simply cannot see.

An LLM doesn’t look facts up. It generates the most plausible continuation of the text in front of it — which is why giving it the right context matters so much.
Key takeaways
  • LLMs generate text one token at a time.
  • Tokens are the unit of limits and pricing.
  • The model only knows what is in its training and its context window.

Module 2Generative AI vs agentic AI

Lesson 3 of 6 · 10 min read

Generative AI: creating from a prompt

Generative AI is AI that creates new content — text, images, code, audio, video — in response to a prompt. You ask, it produces, you decide what to do with the result.

What it is good at

  • Drafting: emails, user stories, release notes, first versions of a spec.
  • Transforming: summarising a transcript, translating, rewriting for a different audience.
  • Extracting and classifying: pulling fields out of an invoice, tagging feedback by theme.
  • Explaining: walking through a concept or a piece of code.

The shape of the interaction

Generative AI is essentially one request, one response. The human stays in control of every step: you read the output, judge it and act on it. That makes it low-risk and easy to adopt — the worst case is a draft you throw away.

Its limit is the same thing: it doesn’t do anything. It can write the Jira ticket, but you still have to create it. It can suggest a SQL query, but it can’t run it and check the result.

Key takeaways
  • Generative AI produces content in response to a prompt.
  • One request, one response — the human acts on the output.
  • Low risk, but it can’t take actions on its own.
Lesson 4 of 6 · 14 min read

Agentic AI: models that plan and act

Agentic AI gives a model the ability to take actions towards a goal. Instead of answering once, an agent works in a loop:

  1. Plan — decide what to do next.
  2. Act — call a tool: search the web, query a database, read a file, run code, create a ticket.
  3. Observe — read the result of that action.
  4. Repeat until the goal is met, then report back.

The model is the same kind of LLM as in generative AI. What changes is the harness around it: a set of tools it is allowed to use and a loop that feeds results back in.

A concrete example

Ask a generative assistant “Is Sprint 24 on track?” and it can only answer from what you paste in. Ask an agent connected to Jira and your QA sheet, and it can fetch the sprint, read the open tickets, check failed tests, and come back with a sourced answer: “Three stories have no acceptance criteria; PAY-112 is blocked by a failed test.”

The autonomy spectrum

Agents range from suggest-only (drafts actions for a human to approve) to fully autonomous (acts without asking). In business settings the sweet spot is usually in between: let the agent read and draft freely, and require human approval for anything irreversible — sending a message, changing a record, spending money.

Key takeaways
  • Agents loop: plan → act with tools → observe → repeat.
  • Same model; the difference is tools plus a loop.
  • Keep humans approving irreversible actions.
Lesson 5 of 6 · 10 min read

Choosing between generative and agentic

Agents are exciting, but they cost more, take longer and can fail in more ways than a single prompt. Start with the simplest thing that works and only move up when you need to.

Three levels to choose from

  • Single call — classification, summarisation, extraction, Q&A over provided text.
  • Workflow — a fixed sequence you design in code (extract → check → draft), with the model doing one step at a time.
  • Agent — open-ended tasks where the steps can’t be known in advance.

Four questions before you build an agent

  1. Complexity: is the task multi-step and hard to fully specify up front?
  2. Value: does the outcome justify the extra cost and latency?
  3. Viability: is the model actually capable at this kind of task?
  4. Cost of error: can mistakes be caught and recovered from — through review, tests or rollback?

If the answer to any of these is “no”, stay at a simpler level. A well-designed workflow beats an unreliable agent every time.

Key takeaways
  • Prefer single call → workflow → agent, in that order.
  • Use the four questions: complexity, value, viability, cost of error.
  • A reliable workflow beats an unreliable agent.

Module 3Using AI responsibly

Lesson 6 of 6 · 12 min read

Limits, risks and human oversight

Every AI system you ship will sometimes be wrong. Responsible AI is mostly about designing for that reality.

The common failure modes

  • Hallucination — confident, plausible, wrong output. Reduce it by grounding the model in real data and asking it to cite sources.
  • Bias — models learn patterns from human data, including unfair ones. Test outputs across different user groups.
  • Privacy and data handling — know what data you send to a model provider, how long it is retained and whether it is used for training.
  • Over-trust — users stop checking once outputs look good. Make evidence visible so checking is easy.

Design patterns that help

  • Cite the source behind every claim, so a human can verify it in one click.
  • Say “I don’t know” when the data doesn’t cover the question, instead of guessing.
  • Ask when uncertain — “Does this test belong to PAY-112 or PAY-118?” costs seconds; a wrong guess can cost a release.
  • Human approval for irreversible actions.

These aren’t limitations bolted on at the end. They are what make people willing to rely on the system at all.

Key takeaways
  • Expect hallucination, bias, privacy and over-trust risks.
  • Ground, cite, admit uncertainty, ask when unsure.
  • Human approval on irreversible actions builds trust.
